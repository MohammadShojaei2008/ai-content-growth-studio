The AI demo that solved the wrong problem

A brand-neutral, approximately four-minute, founder-led educational video script. It demonstrates how to move from a flashy product demo to a customer's real problem. All people, examples, and scenarios below are fictional.

Three opening hook options

01 / Conflict: “Our AI demo looked incredible. The customer still didn't want it. Here's what we missed.”

02 / Curiosity: “What if the most impressive part of your AI product is the part nobody needs?”

03 / Contrarian: “If your AI demo needs ten features to impress someone, you might be hiding the one problem that matters.”

Founder-led narration script

0:00–0:31 | The impressive demo

Picture this: you're showing someone an AI tool you've spent months building. It turns a long meeting transcript into a neat summary in seconds. Decisions, bullet points, action items — all lined up on screen. You hit the last slide, expecting the customer to say, “Where do I sign?”

Instead, they ask one question: “Okay, but who makes sure anyone actually follows up?”

And suddenly, the whole demo feels different.

0:31–1:12 | The problem behind the feature

Let's call the customer Maya. She runs a small team, and she isn't drowning in a lack of summaries. Her team already writes notes. What breaks is everything that happens afterward. Two people walk away thinking the other person owns the task. An important decision sits in a document nobody opens. A deadline arrives with no clear handoff.

The software showed Maya something technically clever. It didn't answer the question she wakes up worrying about: “How do I know the next step won't disappear?”

1:12–1:58 | The wrong question

This is the trap in a lot of product storytelling. We ask, “Which feature will look most impressive on camera?” Then we spend the first minute clicking through buttons and describing capabilities.

But the viewer is asking a different question: “Will this make a real part of my day easier?” Those questions can lead to completely different videos.

Imagine changing just the first scene. Instead of opening on the product, open on a team meeting that ends with one unresolved question. Three people have notes. Nobody has ownership. Now the viewer can feel the cost of the problem before hearing about a solution.

1:58–2:52 | Rebuild the demonstration

Here's the story I'd test. First, show the missed handoff. Second, ask what caused it. Was the decision unclear? Was there no task owner? Was the deadline never confirmed? Third, demonstrate one specific product workflow that might address that exact issue — but only if the real product can actually do it.

That last part matters. If the tool only generates summaries, don't pretend it tracks accountability. Show the summary feature honestly and explain where a person or another system still needs to take over.

A believable demo can include a limitation. In fact, a limitation may be the moment viewers begin to trust the rest of the explanation.

2:52–3:38 | The payoff and next experiment

Now return to Maya's original question: “Who follows up?” The answer doesn't have to be a magical AI assistant. It can be a clear owner, a visible task, and a next check-in — supported by a tool where it truly adds value.

To find out whether this story works, test two openings with the same audience: one starts with the product feature; the other starts with the missed handoff. Look at where viewers stop watching, but also whether the right people understand the value and ask useful follow-up questions. One viral clip isn't proof that you found the right story.

3:38–4:02 | Close

So before you record your next AI demo, write down the customer's most frustrating moment. Then ask: “What must the viewer understand before a feature would actually matter?”

Start there. The feature is not the story. The change in someone's day is.

Production and editorial notes

Visual rhythm: Start with a polished demo screen, interrupt it with the customer's question, then cut to a simple three-person handoff diagram.

Moment of contrast: Side-by-side storyboards: “What the feature does” versus “What the customer needs to know.”

Payoff: Revisit the unanswered ownership question and show an accountable, human-readable next step.

Fact-check gate: Before using this for a real product, verify each capability, integration, permission, pricing implication, and suggested outcome against current documentation or a test account.

Measurement: Evaluate qualified inquiries and message comprehension alongside watch-time; do not promise specific conversion or retention lifts.

The scenario, customer Maya, and hypothetical software demonstration are fictional. No company performance, customer outcome, or product capability is represented as an actual case study. This is original speculative portfolio work, produced with AI-assisted drafting and editorial review.
